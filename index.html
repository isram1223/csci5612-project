Introduction

How community conditions, mental health, and access to care relate to maternal and infant health across the United States.

Background

The health of both mothers and infants can reflect the health of a whole community, in terms of the conditions, mental health, and access to care. Specifically, looking at low birth weight, a baby born less than 5 pounds, 8 ounces [1]. Although a low birthweight is not ideal, about 1 in 12 babies born in the U.S. is born at a low birthweight, this is about 8% [1]. Babies born at a low birthweight may have trouble eating, gaining weight, and fighting infections, this makes weight a very important factor in the health of an infant [1]. Further, these babies are more likely to develop chronic conditions including diabetes, heart disease, and high blood pressure [1]. This is an ongoing issue with 8.2% of babies born in the U.S. in 2016 being born at a low birthweight [2].In 2024, the number increased to 8.5%, proving this issue is not being solved [2]. More specifically in 2024, Black babies were born most at a low birthweight with 15% while for White babies were least likely to be born at a low birthweight with 7% [2]. This is not directly caused by being a person of color, rather because of racism increasing the risk of complications during pregnancy [1]. Complications do not just arise during pregnancy for the mother, about 13% of U.S. women report depressive symptoms after giving birth, showing the need for ongoing care postpartum [3]. These rates range from about 1 in 10 showing rates of depression after giving birth in Illinois to nearly 1 in 4 in Mississippi [3]. However, only 1 in 8 women from the same survey claimed they were not asked about feelings of depression during their postpartum care [3].

There are 5 specific social determinants of health, economic stability, education access and quality, health care access and quality, neighborhood and build environment, and social and community context, these factors all come together to create your community and impact your quality of life [4]. Healthcare access is a very important determinant of health for woman of reproduce age, however 1 in 9 women in this category are uninsured with the highest rates in the South, rural areas, and maternity care deserts [5]. Maternity care deserts are classified as counties with no obstetric clinical sor birthing facilities, and about 1 in 3 U.S. counties classify as maternity care deserts [5]. Additionally, even if there is a hospital, more than half of U.S. counties lack a hospital with labor and delivery services, a very necessary department [5]. Furthermore, the women in these counties must travel further to receive labor and delivery care. This is associated with delayed prenatal care, higher maternal illness, and more NICU stays, all possibly avoidable with closer care [5]. This issue is not getting any better, about 96 labor and delivery units closed between January 2024 and March 2026 [5]. In about 60% of counties affected, this was the only local birthing facility [5]. With all the closures, there has been some strides to improve maternal and infant care. Specifically, Medicaid expansion and postpartum Medicaid extension have expanded coverage [5]. March of Dimes also operates mobile health centers to bring care to those communities with limited access [5]. There is still much more research that needs to be done, the social determinants of health for those in different communities varies widely, and it is still not clear what is most affecting mothers and babies.

Questions

Ten questions guide the project.

  1. Do communities with higher child poverty tend to have more babies born at low birth weight?
  2. Do communities with more uninsured residents tend to have poorer infant health?
  3. Do communities where more adults report frequent mental distress or depression tend to have more babies born at low birth weight?
  4. Do communities with higher poverty or unemployment tend to have more adults who report frequent mental distress?
  5. Are communities with more primary care doctors and mental health providers associated with better infant health and fewer adults reporting frequent mental distress?
  6. Do communities with higher teen birth rates tend to have more babies born at low birth weight?
  7. Are communities where more recent mothers are unmarried also communities with higher poverty and more babies born at low birth weight?
  8. How do metro, small-city, and rural communities differ in infant health, mental health, and access to care?
  9. Which community conditions, such as income, education, insurance, access to care, and racial and ethnic makeup, are most closely linked to infant health?
  10. Are there distinct types of communities, based on their economic, health, and care conditions, and do those types differ in infant health?
